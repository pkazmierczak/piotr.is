This month I'll be concluding an almost year-long experiment in using an iPad Pro as my main, personal computing device. Why would I conduct such a cray experiment, you ask? Let me walk you through my reasoning.



1. I have a standard-issued Macbook Pro from work. It's a work computer, you know, for work. It has my work stuff on it. This means two things: firstly, I don't want to mix my personal stuff with my work stuff for security and compliance reasons, and secondly, I don't want to be tempted to answer that quick email on a Saturday night, or do a “small refactoring”, because we all know how [these end](https://www.monkeyuser.com/2017/refactor-man/).
2. I'd wanted to become a person that doesn't hack on things after work. I don't really need to code in my spare time. I could read, write, edit photographs and make music on my iPad easily. Ah yes, and the "occasional Netflix."
3. Unmatched portability, battery life and, you know, I'd be [one of those people](https://twitter.com/viticci) that use the iPad as their primary device.

Finally, we live in times where Apple's laptops aren't really that tempting, at least not the way they used to be. In 2014, the last time I was buying a personal laptop, there was virtually no competition to the magnificent 13'' Macbook Air. It was pricey, but not much more expensive than equivalent laptops from more, shall we say, prestigious brands. It had by far the best battery life, the best display, the best touchpad, the best keyboard, the best power adapter (omg MagSafe saved my laptops so many times), and the best operating system. Fast forward to 2018, and Macbook Pros are insanely expensive and have the worst keyboards ever put in a laptop. Suddenly it turns out that Windows 10 ships with WSL and [is apparently cool](https://char.gd/blog/category/guides/switching-to-windows), iPad OS is a thing, Chrome OS ships with [Crostini](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromiumos/docs/+/master/containers_and_vms.md)—suddenly there's a myriad of alternatives to the tried and true Apple laptop. I'm not at the stage I'd try Windows yet, so I figured I'd give the iPad Pro a try.

It wasn't easy at first. iPadOS wasn't officially released until Autumn 2019, and iOS 12 wasn't that great with file management, multitasking and many other things. It took me a while to find the right setup when it comes to case/keyboard/stand (and possibilities are _[plentiful](https://www.macstories.net/ipad-diaries/ipad-diaries-the-many-setups-of-the-2018-ipad-pro/)_), and it took me even more to setup [a cloud compute instance](https://github.com/pkazmierczak/aws-cloudformation-templates/blob/master/box.yaml) for the occasional coding, but in the end it worked.

So yeah, you _can_, if you absolutely have to. If it's your main use case for the iPad, you'll be living a rather miserable life with lots of tradeoffs. But it's not all bad. Actually, I learned quite a lot about my personal computing needs and usage patterns in the year that I've been mostly relying on the iPad Pro. 

For example, I learned that I really appreciate a nice screen. My previous experiment involved a second hand Thinkpad running Linux (you know, because 2017 was the year of the Linux desktop), and in comparison to that, the new iPad Pro's soooper fast refresh rate, "true tone" technology, color reproduction and brightness of the display are quite amazing. In fact even compared to the current generation Macbook Pro the expensive iPad holds its own pretty well. I also learned that I appreciate the polish of iOS and its awesome 3rd party app ecosystem. Other than that I guess I also appreciated the portability, battery life, etc.—all the things you can read about in just about any iPad Pro review. But that brings me on to things I didn't like about the platform, and things I realized are perhaps amazing, but just not relevant for me. 

A year with the iPad taught me I'm not as artsy as I'd like to be. Or perhaps I am, but I am bad at putting "creative" apps to good use. So while I do enjoy taking photographs, and I am meticulous at organizing and cataloguing them, I fail to do any photo editing that would yield better results than pressing that "auto" button in Lightroom CC, and for that you don't need an iPad, or a high-end laptop; any smartphone will do. Same goes for any music production related software. I can play music, and I can model my sound with pedals and all kinds of electronic gizmos that my signal passes through, but the rule here is: any button I need to press has to be made of metal. Or plastic. In any case it has to be real. Virtual buttons don't work for me when it comes to music, despite many hours spent on trying to learn the amazing tools that the App Store has to offer.
